Basics & standards

OSHA fall arrest limits, ANSI/ASSP Z359 classes, fall clearance, swing falls, rescue and inspection — a plain-language summary that points you to the real documents.

1. When fall protection is required

  • Construction: under 29 CFR 1926.501, a worker on a surface with an unprotected side or edge 6 feet or more above a lower level must be protected by guardrails, a safety net, or a personal fall arrest system (with specific rules and exceptions for leading edges, holes, roofs and other situations).
  • General industry: under 29 CFR 1910.28, the trigger for unprotected sides and edges is 4 feet or more above a lower level, and the options include guardrails, nets, or personal fall protection (fall arrest, travel restraint, or positioning).
  • Other OSHA subparts (scaffolds, ladders, steel erection, aerial lifts and more) set their own rules. Check the subpart that covers the work.

Personal gear is usually the last line of defense: removing the hazard, guardrails, and travel restraint (which keeps a worker from reaching the edge at all) are generally preferred over arresting a fall after it starts.

2. OSHA personal fall arrest limits (construction)

Paraphrasing 29 CFR 1926.502(d), a personal fall arrest system in construction must, among other things:

ItemKey number (paraphrased)
AnchorageSupports at least 5,000 lb per worker attached — or is designed, installed and used as part of a complete system with a safety factor of at least two, under the supervision of a qualified person.
Maximum arresting force1,800 lb on the worker when used with a body harness (body belts are not allowed for fall arrest).
Free fallRigged so the worker cannot free fall more than 6 ft, and cannot hit any lower level.
Deceleration distanceStops the worker within 3.5 ft of deceleration distance.
D-rings and snap hooksMinimum tensile strength 5,000 lb, proof-tested to 3,600 lb without cracking, breaking or permanent deformation. Only locking snap hooks may be used.
Lanyards and vertical lifelinesMinimum breaking strength 5,000 lb; each worker on a vertical lifeline needs their own line (narrow exception for elevator shafts).
Horizontal lifelinesDesigned, installed and used under the supervision of a qualified person, keeping a safety factor of at least two.
Harness attachmentCenter of the back near shoulder level, or above the head.
After a fallEquipment that took an impact load comes out of service immediately until a competent person inspects it and decides it is undamaged and fit for reuse.
Inspection and rescueInspect before each use; remove defective parts. There must be a plan for prompt rescue, or workers must be able to rescue themselves.

Also note: OSHA’s Appendix C test criteria for (d)(16) assume a combined worker-plus-tool weight under 310 lb; heavier users need adjusted criteria. That is one reason capacity ratings above 310 lb are called out in the chart.

3. General industry (1910.140) differences

29 CFR 1910.140 covers personal fall protection systems outside construction. The core performance numbers match construction — 1,800 lb maximum arresting force, 3.5 ft maximum deceleration distance, 6 ft free fall with no contact with a lower level, 5,000 lb anchorages (or safety factor of two under a qualified person), 5,000 lb connector strength proof-tested to 3,600 lb. Points worth knowing:

  • It states a gate strength of 3,600 lb for snap hooks and carabiners, and requires them to be the automatic-locking type.
  • Free fall may exceed 6 ft if the manufacturer designed and tested the system for it and the 1,800 lb force limit is still met — this is how 12 ft free-fall lanyards fit in.
  • The harness attachment may be at the front (sternal) if free fall is limited to 2 ft or less.
  • Gear must be inspected before initial use during each work shift.

4. ANSI/ASSP Z359 and CSA Z259 at a glance

The ANSI/ASSP Z359 Fall Protection Code is a family of voluntary consensus standards that most U.S. gear is designed and labelled to; OSHA is the law, Z359 is the product and program standard most makers test against. The family is maintained by ASSP (ASSP Z359 overview). Canada’s equivalent product standards are the CSA Z259 series (CSA Group standards), which is why some rows list both. A few parts you will see in the chart:

  • Z359.11 — full-body harnesses. Harnesses are labelled with classes by attachment point: A (fall arrest, dorsal D-ring — every fall arrest harness), D (suspension / controlled descent), E (confined space entry and retrieval, usually shoulder attachments), L (ladder climbing, front attachment) and P (work positioning, side D-rings at the hips). A harness can carry several letters, e.g. “A, P”.
  • Z359.13 — energy absorbers and energy-absorbing lanyards. Rated by maximum free fall: 6 ft free-fall units for tie-off at or above the D-ring, and 12 ft free-fall units for tie-off down to foot level. The 12 ft versions allow more stretch, so they need more clearance below.
  • Z359.14 — self-retracting devices. Class 1 SRLs are meant for anchors overhead (at or above the dorsal D-ring). Class 2 SRLs are also tested for anchorages lower than that, where the free fall and arrest distance are longer. Device types include SRL, SRL-R (with an integral rescue / retrieval function), SRL-P (personal, worn on the harness), and SRL-LE (leading edge: tested for the line running over a sharp edge).
  • Z359.12 — connecting components. Snap hooks, carabiners and similar connectors must be self-closing and self-locking, with gates rated to 3,600 lbf — the “Gate lbf” column in the Connectors chart.
  • Other parts cover program management, anchorages, horizontal lifelines, rescue systems, ladder systems and training. Read the current edition: this summary simplifies.

5. Fall clearance

Fall clearance is how much open space must exist below the working surface (or below the anchor) so a falling worker stops before hitting anything. Add up:

clearance = free fall + deceleration distance + harness stretch / D-ring shift + D-ring-to-feet height + safety margin

  • Free fall: how far the D-ring drops before the system starts to slow it. With a 6 ft lanyard tied at D-ring height, that is about 6 ft; tied at foot level it can be close to 12 ft.
  • Deceleration distance: how far the energy absorber or SRL brake extends while stopping the fall (OSHA caps it at 3.5 ft; many products publish their own figure).
  • Harness stretch and D-ring shift: the harness webbing stretches and the D-ring slides up the back under load.
  • Worker height: the distance from the D-ring down to the soles of the feet.
  • Safety margin: extra room for the unknowns.

Illustrative example only (round numbers, not a design). 6 ft energy-absorbing lanyard anchored at D-ring height: 6 ft free fall + 3.5 ft deceleration (the OSHA maximum) + 1 ft harness stretch and D-ring shift + 5 ft from D-ring to feet + 2 ft margin ≈ 17.5 ft below the anchor.

That is why a 6 ft lanyard often does not work for low work, and why SRLs — which typically lock within a short distance — are common when clearance is tight. Always use the manufacturer’s clearance chart for the exact product and anchor position.

Why Class 2 and leading-edge setups need more room: when the anchor is at foot level or below the D-ring, the worker falls further before the line is taut and the device locks, and over an edge the line also bends and rubs. Makers publish larger clearance figures for these cases — often with extra allowance for swing.

6. Swing falls and leading edges

If the anchor is not directly overhead, a falling worker swings like a pendulum back toward the line under the anchor. The swing can slam them into a column or the structure, and the arc also adds to the vertical distance fallen. Keep work close to the area under the anchor, move the anchor as you move (trolleys, horizontal lifelines), and treat the maker’s swing-fall limits as part of clearance. Where the line may contact an edge (roof decks, steel, slab edges), use equipment rated for leading-edge use and follow its setback and edge-angle rules.

7. Suspension and rescue planning

Arresting the fall is only half the job. A worker hanging in a harness can develop suspension intolerance (often called suspension trauma), in which leg straps restrict circulation and the worker can lose consciousness. OSHA requires prompt rescue; in practice that means a written plan, gear and trained people before work starts, not a 911 call afterward.

  • Plan how a fallen worker will be reached: ladders or lifts, an SRL with a rescue function, a pre-rigged rescue or descent kit, or professional rescue that can actually arrive in time.
  • Suspension relief straps let a conscious worker stand in a loop and take pressure off the legs while waiting.
  • Rescued workers should be medically evaluated.

8. Inspection and retirement

  • Before each use: the user checks webbing and stitching (cuts, burns, fraying, chemical or UV damage, broken stitches), hardware (cracks, deformation, corrosion, gates that do not close and lock), labels, and any impact or fall indicators.
  • Periodic inspection: ANSI expects documented inspection by a competent person at intervals set by the program and the maker (commonly at least yearly; harsher use means more often). SRLs may also need factory service.
  • Retire immediately any gear that has arrested a fall or shows an activated impact indicator, until a competent person decides otherwise per the manufacturer — most makers say retire.
  • Some manufacturers set a service life for textile gear; others rely on inspection. Follow the instructions for your product.
  • Never hoist materials with personal fall protection, and never tie off to a guardrail or anything not rated as an anchorage.

Frequently asked questions

What weight can a full-body harness hold?

ANSI Z359.11 harnesses are rated by the maker for a user weight range, meaning the worker plus clothing and tools (ASSP Z359 overview). OSHA’s Appendix C test criteria for 1926.502(d)(16) assume a combined weight under 310 lb, and heavier users need adjusted criteria (1926.502). Check the harness label and the maker’s instructions. The Harnesses chart lists each model’s published capacity.

What is the OSHA free-fall limit?

Under 1926.502(d)(16), a personal fall arrest system must be rigged so the worker cannot free fall more than 6 ft or hit a lower level, with a maximum arresting force of 1,800 lb and a maximum deceleration distance of 3.5 ft. 1910.140 allows a free fall of more than 6 ft only if the manufacturer designed and tested the system for it and the 1,800 lb limit is still met.

When is fall protection required?

In construction, at 6 feet or more above a lower level (1926.501). In general industry, at 4 feet or more (1910.28). Other OSHA subparts, such as scaffolds and ladders, set their own rules, so check the one that covers the work.

Should I use a lanyard or an SRL?

An energy-absorbing lanyard has a fixed length and an absorber rated for a 6 ft or 12 ft free fall under Z359.13. An SRL pays out and retracts and typically locks within a short distance, so it often needs less clearance. Class 1 SRLs are meant for overhead anchors; Class 2 are also tested for lower anchorages (ASSP Z359 overview). Whichever you pick, use the maker’s clearance chart. Compare the Lanyards and SRLs charts.

What should I do with gear after a fall?

Take it out of service immediately. Under 1926.502(d)(19), equipment that took an impact load stays out until a competent person inspects it and decides it is undamaged and fit for reuse. Most makers say to retire it, and an activated impact indicator means remove it.

How does ANSI Z359 differ from OSHA?

OSHA is the law. ANSI/ASSP Z359 is a family of voluntary consensus standards that most U.S. gear is designed and labelled to (ASSP Z359 overview). In Canada the equivalent product standards are CSA Z259 (CSA Group).

How often should fall protection gear be inspected?

Before each use (1926.502(d)(21)); 1910.140 says before initial use during each work shift. ANSI expects a documented inspection by a competent person at intervals set by the program and the maker, commonly at least yearly (ASSP Z359 overview).

What about rescue after a fall?

OSHA requires prompt rescue, or workers who can rescue themselves (1926.502(d)(20)). A worker hanging in a harness is at risk of suspension intolerance, so the plan, gear and trained people must be ready before work starts. Suspension relief straps help while waiting, and rescued workers should be medically evaluated. See Suspension and rescue planning and the Rescue & descent chart.

How strong does an anchor need to be?

An anchorage must support at least 5,000 lb per worker attached, or be designed, installed and used as part of a complete system with a safety factor of at least two, under a qualified person (1926.502(d)). See the Anchors chart for anchorage connectors.
